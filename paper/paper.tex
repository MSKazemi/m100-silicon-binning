\begin{abstract}
Vendors sell large dies as lower-core-count parts by disabling cores, defective or not,
without saying which. On CINECA's Marconi100 the baseboard management controller
exposes one thermal sensor per core position without renumbering survivors, so the positions that
never report form each die's \emph{harvest map}. From public telemetry we recover maps for 1{,}962
POWER9 sockets over 1.67 million socket-days. In every map the eight disabled cores of the 24-core
die form four complete \emph{slices}, core pairs sharing an L2/L3 block, though the hardware can
disable single cores; 443 of 495 possible patterns occur, varying from die to die.
Disabled slices cluster above all within the six \emph{quads}, slice pairs adjacent in IBM's chip
diagram, where thermal coupling is also strongest. A sharp boundary at
rack~22, predictable out of sample, separates two populations of parts with different disable
statistics, most plausibly procurement lots. An independent boot signal places a boot inside 133
of 136 steady-state map changes, and none without one. Nine steady-state core-guard
episodes, removing a slice or core at boot, are each matched by the operating system's own CPU
count.
Maps travel with their dies, so map changes trace processors between nodes: sockets change die at
2.1\,\% per year, new processors entering at an inferred 1.0--1.2\,\%. Equivalence tests bound the map's
association with socket power to a few watts, and it does not usefully predict replacement. The disclosure lies in
which sensors exist, not their values, so coarser sampling does not remove it.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Large processor dies are susceptible to manufacturing defects~\cite{koren1993negbinom}, and a die
whose faulty core is disabled can still be sold~\cite{shivakumar2003redundancy,powell2009salvaging};
vendors also disable working units to fill cheaper products~\cite{deneckere1996damaged,mccalpin2021knl,ibm_p9um}.
Either way the die ships as a lower-core-count part. We call this \emph{silicon
harvesting}, or \emph{die harvesting}, to distinguish it from the now more common architectural
sense of ``core harvesting'' as lending idle cores \emph{temporarily} to co-located
work~\cite{hardharvest2025}; the two are unrelated.
The practice is openly acknowledged, but the resulting \emph{per-die} maps of which units were
disabled are not published: a buyer is told the part has 16 cores, not \emph{which} 16, and the
fleet-wide readouts we know of ran code on each node~\cite{mccalpin2021knl,mccalpin2021capid}.
Those maps carry information about a vendor's defect and binning process and about a fleet's
provenance that vendors do not publish.

Independently, HPC operators have begun publishing holistic out-of-band monitoring at fleet
scale~\cite{borghesi2023m100}. Such telemetry carries an unintended side channel:
a baseboard management controller exposes one thermal sensor per core position on the die and
does not renumber the surviving cores compactly, so the sensors that never report mark exactly the
disabled positions, the die's harvest map. The owner of one POWER9 machine can read this from its
sensors~\cite{smith2020p9layout}; published fleet telemetry discloses it for every die at once.

We exploit this to recover harvest maps for 1{,}962 of the 1{,}968 processor sockets of CINECA's
Marconi100, a Top-10 system, from a public dataset, and to track them across 858 days. Our contributions are:

\begin{itemize}
\item A recovery method (Sec.~\ref{sec:method}) with an explicit validity argument, needing only
      per-core sensor telemetry that operators already collect, \textbf{validated against the
      operating system's and the scheduler's own core counts} (Sec.~\ref{sec:groundtruth}).
\item To our knowledge the first characterisation of harvest maps recovered from
      \emph{published} out-of-band telemetry, without access to the machines: fleet-scale, and
      tracked daily over 31 months. On these 16-core parts every disabled core lies in a fully
      disabled POWER9 slice, maps are per-die rather than per-SKU, and harvested slices cluster,
      above all within the quads IBM documents.
\item Evidence of rack-aligned \emph{lot structure} from telemetry alone: a sharp changepoint,
      predictable for held-out racks, separating two populations of parts with markedly different
      binning statistics, most plausibly two procurement lots.
\item A longitudinal result over 1.67\,M socket-days: maps are near-static, configuration changes
      coincide with reboots \textbf{measured against an independent boot signal} rather than
      inferred from reporting gaps (Sec.~\ref{par:reboot}), and field core-guard episodes are
      isolated, the nine in steady state each matched by the OS's own CPU count.
\item A taxonomy of map changes by the event each is consistent with (Sec.~\ref{sec:taxonomy}),
      which also traces processors moved between nodes and so separates a 2.1\,\% annual die-change
      rate from an inferred 1.0--1.2\,\% rate of new processors, with no asset database.
\item Two bounded nulls that matter operationally: an equivalence analysis bounding the map's
      association with socket power to a few watts, below the spread between sockets
      (Sec.~\ref{sec:impact}), and a survival model finding no useful prediction of
      field replacement (Sec.~\ref{sec:reliability}).
\item A quantification of the disclosure itself (Sec.~\ref{sec:channel}): a single 20\,s
      sampling interval recovers the exact map for 99.95\,\% of socket-intervals, and neither
      reducing resolution nor random deletion short of destroying the data mitigates it.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{The machine, from room to core}
Marconi100's 980 compute nodes occupy 49 rack cabinets of 20 nodes each, arranged in three rows of
the machine room, with $\mathrm{id}=20\,\mathrm{rack}+\mathrm{slot}$ and every rack's $(X,Y)$
floor position documented~\cite{exadata_repo}. Each node is an AC922 8335-GTG chassis carrying
\emph{two} CPU sockets and four V100 GPUs. Each socket holds one POWER9 die; each die carries 24
SMT4 core positions organised as 12 slices; each slice is two cores plus their shared L2 and L3.
Fig.~\ref{fig:hier} shows the chain. Two consequences matter throughout: the unit we recover a map
for is a \emph{socket}, two per node rather than one, and the unit disabled on these parts is a \emph{slice},
not a core. Sec.~\ref{sec:pops} fixes the exact socket populations.

\subsection{POWER9 slice organisation}
The POWER9 die used in the IBM Power System AC922 contains 24 SMT4 cores (equivalently 12 SMT8
cores) on a $695\,\mathrm{mm}^2$ die in 14\,nm FinFET with 17 metal layers~\cite{gonzalez2017power9}.
Critically, \emph{IBM groups each pair of SMT4 cores with a 512\,kB L2 and a 10\,MB L3 into a
processor-pair cache slice}~\cite[pp.~30, 155]{ibm_p9um}, an EX in its firmware~\cite{hostboot_p9}.
We call this unit a \emph{slice}; IBM also uses the word for the execution slices inside a
core~\cite[slide~7]{thompto2016power9}, which we never mean. IBM's Hot Chips 28 presentation shows
the 24 cores above 12 $\times$ 10\,MB L3 blocks, attached to a 7\,TB/s on-chip fabric at
$256\,\mathrm{GB/s}\times12$~\cite[slides~4, 11]{thompto2016power9}. Two slices form a
\emph{quad}. A bad L2 disables both cores of its slice~\cite[pp.~263--264]{ibm_p9regs}, but a
single core can also be deconfigured~\cite[p.~311]{ibm_p9um}, and an 8-core POWER9 part runs one
core in each of eight slices~\cite{smith2020p9layout}; whether these parts disable whole slices is
therefore an empirical question (Sec.~\ref{sec:pops}).

Marconi100 nodes are AC922 model 8335-GTG with $2\times$ 16-core POWER9 at 2.6/3.1\,GHz and
$4\times$ NVIDIA V100~\cite{ibm_ac922,cineca_m100,top500_m100}. This model uses the 24-core
scale-out POWER9 die in 16- and 20-core versions~\cite{ibm_ac922}, so \textbf{a 16-core part is a
24-core die with eight cores disabled}; which eight is the object of our study. IBM documents two
routes to a disabled core~\cite[p.~311]{ibm_p9um}: at each boot the firmware deconfigures cores
with manufacturing defects, read from partial-good data in the module's VPD~\cite{hostboot_p9}, and
it can deconfigure working cores not licensed for use, e.g.\ through a factory core-count
override~\cite{ibm_deconfig}. Telemetry shows \emph{which} positions are disabled, not which route
disabled them. Fig.~\ref{fig:schematic} shows the organisation annotated with the harvest rates we
measure.

\subsection{The M100 ExaData dataset}
M100 ExaData~\cite{borghesi2023m100} publishes 49.9\,TB of telemetry from Marconi100 covering
2020-03 to 2022-09 across 980+ nodes; we use all twelve monthly-archive
releases~\cite{m100_zenodo}. The IPMI plugin collects BMC sensor data at 20\,s cadence.
The documentation describes the core-temperature family as \texttt{pX\_coreY\_temp}, ``Temperature
of core n.\ Y in the CPU socket n.\ X. X=0..1, Y=0..23''~\cite{exadata_repo}. \emph{This specifies
the BMC sensor-ID namespace, not the active core count}. That distinction is the entry point for
this study.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}
\subsection{Recovering the disable map}
For each month we enumerate the \texttt{metric=pX\_coreY\_temp} partitions and count samples per
(node, socket, core). A core index is \emph{present} for a socket if it emits any sample in the
window. The disable map of socket $s$ is $M_s\in\{0,1\}^{12}$ over slices
$k=\lfloor \mathrm{core}/2 \rfloor$, with $M_{s,k}=1$ when neither core of slice $k$ reports (the
slice is disabled); every socket analysed has $\sum_k M_{s,k}=4$. We call $M_s$ the socket's
\emph{harvest map}; it records which positions are disabled, not why.

The key validity argument is that \textbf{the BMC does not renumber active cores compactly}: were
that so, every socket would report indices 0--15. Instead we observe arbitrary 16-element subsets
of $\{0,\dots,23\}$, so the index is fixed to the core rather than to its rank among the
survivors; IBM's numbering ties it to a position on the die (Sec.~\ref{sec:layout}).

\subsection{External validation of the premise}
\label{sec:groundtruth}
Everything in this paper rests on one premise: that the per-core sensors that never report are
exactly the die's disabled positions. The argument of Sec.~\ref{sec:method} is internal: a renumbering BMC would
yield one pattern, and we see 443. We can do better, because M100 ExaData carries two collectors
that never touch the IPMI sensor family and that both know how many cores exist.

\textbf{The operating system.} The Ganglia plugin reports \texttt{cpu\_num}, the node's own
logical-CPU count. POWER9 runs four SMT threads per core, so two sockets of 16 cores should present
$2\times16\times4=128$ logical CPUs. Across the whole record (\textbf{847{,}297{,}478 samples,
990 nodes, the 29 months in which Ganglia collected this metric}), the OS reports exactly 128 on
\textbf{99.9853\,\%} of samples. Restricted to the single month 2022-08 used for the thermal work
(40.9\,M samples, 988 nodes) the figure is \textbf{99.997\,\%}. We give both because both appear
below, and they are different windows rather than different measurements of the same one.

\textbf{The scheduler.} SLURM's configured-CPU totals across the four partitions are
$110{,}208+12{,}672+2{,}560+256=125{,}696$, which is $982\times128$. The scheduler agrees, from a
third independent path.

\textbf{The one exception is itself a confirmation.} Exactly one node deviates \emph{in the month
2022-08}: node 145 reports 124 logical CPUs, one core short, across 2022-08-11--12; over the
same two days the BMC loses exactly one core sensor on socket p1 from a reboot, and the socket then
returns with a different map (the slice at cores $\{22,23\}$ replaced by $\{18,19\}$) while socket
p0 is unchanged all month. Two collectors with nothing in common flag the same node, and the same
number of cores, in the same 48 hours. This ties the sensor-presence signal to real
hardware configuration rather than to an artefact of the monitoring stack.

The premise is therefore externally confirmed at fleet scale for the \emph{count}, by the OS and the
scheduler independently; \emph{which} positions are disabled rests on the non-compact indexing above
and on Secs.~\ref{sec:pops} and~\ref{sec:layout}.

\subsection{Pipeline, filters, and what each filter protects against}
\label{sec:pipeline}
The recovery above is one line of logic; making it reliable at 1.67\,M socket-days is mostly a
question of filters, and we state them here rather than introducing them where they are first used.

\emph{Stage 1: extraction.} Each monthly archive is scanned once and only the 48
\texttt{p\{0,1\}\_core\{0..23\}\_temp} partitions are extracted. A \textbf{completeness gate}
refuses to emit a month unless all 48 are present. This is not defensive boilerplate: a partial
extraction is indistinguishable, downstream, from a socket with disabled cores, and an early
version of this pipeline produced exactly that artefact for three months (Sec.~\ref{sec:failure}).

\emph{Stage 2: daily aggregation.} Samples are reduced to a count per (node, socket, core,
day), giving a 15\,MB table from a 400\,GB scan. Every structural and longitudinal result in this
paper is computed from that table; only the thermal analysis returns to the raw series.

\emph{Stage 3: the clean-day filter.} A socket-day is \emph{clean} when exactly 16 cores report
\emph{and} each reports more than 90\,\% of the 4{,}320 samples expected at 20\,s cadence. This
is the load-bearing choice in the paper, so its effect is stated plainly: it retains
1{,}517{,}473 of 1{,}671{,}297 socket-days (90.8\,\%), and Sec.~\ref{sec:lot} shows the lot
result is unchanged when the threshold is tightened to sockets with $\ge$90\,\% clean-day
coverage. The filter exists because a partially-collected day mimics a configuration change.
Because it conditions on a count of 16, it cannot reveal a configuration of another size; the
census of every other socket-day in Sec.~\ref{sec:lifecycle} finds only short deconfiguration
episodes and partially sampled days.

\emph{Stage 4: per-socket map.} A socket is summarised by the modal harvest map over its clean
days (median 775 days supporting each map). Sockets whose modal map does not have exactly four
harvested slices are excluded; this removes 6 of 1{,}968. For the 206 sockets whose map changes,
the modal map is that of one die, so time-dependent analyses use the map in force at the time.

\emph{Cost.} One full pass is a 400\,GB sequential scan, roughly two hours on a single node;
re-running every result from the published derived table takes minutes.

\subsection{Failure modes of the method}
\label{sec:failure}
Four things could make this recovery wrong, and each has a detector.

\textbf{Partial ingestion.} If archive extraction silently truncates, absent metrics look like
harvested cores. \emph{Detector:} the 48-metric completeness gate. We found this the hard way:
three months were once written from incomplete extractions, one of them missing all 24 sensors of
socket p1, which would have read as a socket with no active cores at all.

\textbf{Compact renumbering.} If a BMC renumbered surviving cores $0..15$, the method would
recover nothing but a constant. \emph{Detector:} we observe arbitrary 16-element subsets of
$\{0,\dots,23\}$ and 443 distinct patterns; a renumbering BMC would produce exactly one.

\textbf{Per-metric collector faults.} A collector that drops individual metric streams mimics
deconfiguration. \emph{Detectors:} odd active-core counts, which no baseline map
produces (39 socket-days; a single-core deconfiguration can, Sec.~\ref{sec:lifecycle}); reduced counts that are strict subsets of the socket's modal map; and,
for the guard episodes specifically, the control that every other sensor on the same socket kept
full cadence (Sec.~\ref{sec:lifecycle}).

\textbf{Socket-tag swaps.} If the collector exchanges the p0/p1 tags, a longitudinal study
silently mixes two dies. \emph{Detector:} the mirror test of Sec.~\ref{sec:taxonomy}, which
identifies 19\,\% of steady-state map changes as relabelling, which moves no die.

\subsection{Null model for clustering}
\label{sec:null}
Every socket has exactly four harvested slices, so the constraint induces negative dependence and a
naive independence null is invalid. We use the curveball algorithm~\cite{strona2014curveball},
which preserves \emph{both} row sums (4 per socket) and column sums (per-slice marginals) while
randomising association structure, and assert both on every draw. The $B=100{,}000$ draws are
exchangeable with the data by Besag and Clifford's parallel construction~\cite{besag1989generalized}
(40 global curveball trades~\cite{carstens2018global} to a hidden matrix, then 40 more for each
draw), so the Monte-Carlo $p=(b+1)/(B+1)$, where $b$ draws are at least as extreme, is valid however
slowly the chain mixes. A $z$ against a simulated null is a Monte-Carlo standardised distance,
$(T-\bar{T}_{\mathrm{null}})/\mathrm{SD}_{\mathrm{null}}$; significance is read from $p$.

\subsection{Thermal probing of die topology}
\label{sec:thermal}
Cores on a socket share workload and inlet air, producing a dominant common mode. We remove it by
subtracting the per-timestamp cross-core mean, double-centre, and compute residual correlations at
the sensors' native 20\,s cadence. No resampling is applied, and Sec.~\ref{par:grid} shows why
that matters. Cores that are neighbours on the die, or share a voltage and frequency domain,
should retain positive residual coupling.

\paragraph*{Caveat} Common-mode removal forces each row of the residual matrix to sum to
approximately zero, so negative values at large index gaps are partly mechanical. We therefore rely
on contrasts \emph{at identical index distance} and on ordering tests, which the constraint cannot
bias: it treats every core alike, so it can rescale an ordering but not create one. Every thermal
interval resamples the 49 racks.

\subsection{Every socket is 16 cores; granularity is the slice}
\paragraph*{Populations}\label{sec:pops} Two socket populations recur and are kept distinct throughout:
$\mathcal{S}_{\text{all}}$, the 1{,}968 sockets appearing anywhere in the record (984 nodes), and
$\mathcal{S}_{\text{map}}$, the 1{,}962 with at least one clean day and hence a well-defined
harvest map. All structural results below are over $\mathcal{S}_{\text{map}}$ and the full 31-month record.
One further distinction matters only for the rack-level analyses: node identities run to 983,
four beyond the 980 that fill the 49 documented cabinets, and those four have no documented
$(X,Y)$ floor position. The 2 sockets of theirs that carry a map are excluded wherever a result
is indexed by rack, leaving 1{,}960; being a four-node group at the end of the id range, they
would otherwise form a degenerate segment that captures the argmax of any unguarded changepoint
scan (Sec.~\ref{par:changepoint}).

The perfect pair structure (Table~\ref{tab:struct}) is the central structural finding: it matches the POWER9 slice
exactly, and no socket's map disables one core of a slice and keeps the other, although the
hardware can deconfigure single cores~\cite[p.~311]{ibm_p9um} and an 8-core POWER9 part runs one
core in each of eight slices~\cite{smith2020p9layout}. Slice granularity is therefore a property of
these 16-core parts, not a constraint of the die; the 4{,}200 Xeon Phi 7250s of another centre
likewise lose whole two-core tiles~\cite{mccalpin2021knl}.


\subsection{The map is per-die, not per-SKU}
\textbf{443} distinct patterns appear among the $\binom{12}{4}=495$ possible
(Fig.~\ref{fig:rank}). Per-die means that the map varies from processor to processor rather than
being fixed by the SKU; POWER9 firmware reads each module's partial-good record from that module's
own vital product data at boot~\cite{hostboot_p9}. The most common map (slices 0--3) covers only
5.5\% of sockets. Within a node the two sockets match in just 1.2\,\% of cases (12 of 981 nodes),
with mean overlap 1.641 slices against a shuffled null of $1.470\pm0.026$ ($z=+6.6$), so they are
nearly independent, with a small excess consistent with same-lot pairing.


\subsection{Marginals are strongly non-uniform}
Slice~0 is harvested on 61.2\% of sockets against a uniform expectation of 33.3\%
($\chi^2=807.2$, $\mathrm{df}=11$, $p\ll0.001$, conservative since each socket contributes four
distinct slices; a Monte-Carlo test with that constraint gives $p=1/(B+1)=5\times10^{-5}$ and a
rack-clustered Wald test $p=3\times10^{-6}$); Fig.~\ref{fig:marg} splits this by lot. These
marginals are stable in time: across the 30 months with clean data the largest standard deviation
of any slice's monthly rate is \textbf{0.25 percentage points}, and slice~0 ranges only
60.9--61.8\% (Fig.~\ref{fig:stab}). Changing composition does not produce this: a fixed cohort of
the 1{,}900 sockets with a clean day in every post-commissioning month has a largest standard
deviation of 0.22 points, and only 0.21\,\% of its socket-months change map from the month before.


\subsection{Harvested slices cluster, above all within quads}
\label{sec:cluster}
Under the curveball null the mean index gap between harvested slices is $4.242$ versus
$4.582\pm0.016$ ($z=-20.7$; none of the 100{,}000 null draws is as small, $p=1/(B+1)\approx10^{-5}$).
Of the $\binom{12}{2}=66$ pair counts, 20 are significant at family-wise error $0.05$, identically
under a Monte-Carlo Bonferroni test ($p\le0.05/66$) and a max-$|z|$ test (Fig.~\ref{fig:cooc}).

Nine are positive excesses and eleven deficits. \emph{Exactly eight} positive excesses have
$|\Delta k|\le2$: the six pairs $(2m,2m{+}1)$, which share a \emph{quad}, POWER9's group of four
cores whose voltage and frequency change together~\cite{ibm_p9um}, plus 1\&2 and 0\&2. The most
significant is 0\&1 ($z=+16.1$); the ninth excess, 7\&10, is at $|\Delta k|=3$. Of the five index
neighbours that straddle two quads only 1\&2 shows an excess; 3\&4, 5\&6, 7\&8 and 9\&10 sit at
$0.89$--$1.09\times$ the null. Nine of the eleven deficits are at $|\Delta k|\ge4$, the exceptions
being 2\&4 and 1\&4. A 10{,}000-draw run of the Besag--Clifford sequential null (Sec.~\ref{sec:null}) reaches the same
66 decisions. Part of the
pattern is lot mixture: with trades restricted to within each lot, so each keeps its own marginals,
the mean-gap $z$ is $-15.4$ and 11 pairs remain, seven of the eight near excesses (0\&2 falls to
$z=+3.1$) and four deficits, all at $|\Delta k|\ge6$.

The six same-quad pairs are also neighbours on the die. IBM's chip diagram places the two slices of
each quad next to each other and consecutive quads in opposite bands~\cite[Fig.~2]{ibm_p9regs}
(Sec.~\ref{sec:layout}), so this part of the clustering is spatial, at the scale of a quad; the
other two near excesses, 1\&2 and 0\&2, join slices in opposite bands of one die column. A pairwise
model with separate adjacency terms inside and across quads puts most of the adjacency inside the
quad ($\gamma=+0.92$ against $+0.28$, Sec.~\ref{par:ppc}). Spatially clustered
defects~\cite{koren1993negbinom,bae2007waferyield} and disabling at quad granularity would both
produce this, and the telemetry cannot tell them apart (OI-3).

\paragraph*{How big, not just how significant} A $z$ is a signal-to-noise ratio and grows with
$\sqrt{n}$, so the values above report sample size as much as effect. Fig.~\ref{fig:cooc}
therefore plots the effect itself, $\log_2$ of observed over expected co-harvest, against slice
distance. The excess is real but moderate: averaged over pairs at $|\Delta k|\le2$ it is
$+0.165$ ($1.12\times$ the null), against $-0.089$ ($0.94\times$) for more distant pairs. The six
largest excesses, $1.32$--$1.73\times$ (strongest 6\&7), are exactly the six same-quad pairs.
Clustering of this size is easy to detect across 1{,}962 sockets and easy to overstate from a $z$
alone, so both are given.

\subsection{Thermal coupling follows the quad, not the slice}
We probe \textbf{every} socket with both halves fully configured, \textbf{1{,}948 sockets across
974 nodes}, over the full 31-day month 2022-08 (Fig.~\ref{fig:thermal}). At \emph{identical}
index distance of~1, within-slice siblings couple more strongly than cross-slice neighbours on
average, $r=+0.372$ against $+0.312$ ($15{,}584$ and $10{,}122$ pairs), a contrast of $+0.061$
(95\,\% CI $[0.047,0.076]$, resampling the 49 racks). That average mixes two kinds of neighbour.
IBM's chip diagram stacks the four cores of each quad in one column and alternates consecutive
quads between the die's two bands~\cite{ibm_p9regs}, so index neighbours in different quads are not
neighbours on the die. Cross-slice neighbours within a quad (55\,\% of them) couple at $r=+0.480$,
those across a quad boundary at $+0.104$. Matched within each socket, siblings couple \emph{less}
than the adjacent core of the other slice in their quad ($-0.102$, CI $[-0.114,-0.089]$) and far
more than cores across a quad boundary ($+0.246$, $[0.227,0.267]$); both keep their sign in each
lot, socket position and month tested, but their mixture does not: it is $-0.004$ ($[-0.019,0.013]$) in
2022-02 and $+0.104$ in 2021-05. The thermal unit is the quad; the slice is the unit of disabling.

\paragraph*{A census, not a sample} Subsampling in \emph{time} rather than in nodes lets the whole
fleet be processed in one pass: we keep every fifth 20\,s sample, which lowers the sample count
without smoothing and so, unlike a coarser alignment grid, does not inflate correlations. The
layout score of Sec.~\ref{sec:layout} moves from $-0.787$ on the 180-socket subsample of
Table~\ref{tab:grid} to $-0.791$ fleet-wide. Every thermal number reported here is a fleet-wide
measurement, and every interval resamples racks: treating the pair correlations as independent, as
an earlier version did, understates the variance of the contrast about five-fold.

\paragraph*{The effect size depends on temporal resolution; the ordering does not}
\label{par:grid}
Cores are sampled every 20\,s and their timestamps coincide across a socket, so no resampling is
required: an inner join at native resolution retains 133{,}907 of 133{,}920 possible rows
(99.99\,\%). Earlier versions of this analysis nonetheless aligned onto a one-minute grid, a step
introduced to work around an unrelated bug and never revisited. It is not neutral
(Table~\ref{tab:grid}): coarser averaging suppresses high-frequency noise and inflates every
correlation, within-slice more than cross-slice, so within this subsample the contrast grows from
$+0.063$ at 20\,s to $+0.140$ at 15\,min. \textbf{We therefore quote the native-resolution figure, which is the most
conservative.} What does \emph{not} move is the ordering (within-slice exceeds cross-slice at
every grid) and the layout score of Sec.~\ref{sec:layout}, which sits at
$-0.787,-0.773,-0.766,-0.767$ across the four grids.

\paragraph*{What couples the cores?} Inside a quad, physical adjacency and a shared operating
point coincide: its four cores are neighbours on the die~\cite{ibm_p9regs} and change voltage and
frequency together~\cite{ibm_p9um}, so their power, and hence their temperature, can move together
without any heat exchange. Lagged correlations cannot separate the two. On-die hot spots evolve over
milliseconds~\cite{skadron2003hotspot}, far faster than the 20\,s sample, while the heat sink
responds over minutes; at true 20\,s lags every kind of neighbour loses roughly a quarter to a half of its
lag-0 coupling within one sample (24\,\% within a slice, 52\,\% across quads) and then changes by
less than 0.02 out to 200\,s.

\subsection{Sensor index and position on the die}
\label{sec:layout}
Is the sensor index merely a label, or does it track physical position? IBM documents the answer.
Its chip diagram labels the core chiplets EC00--EC23~\cite[Fig.~2, p.~33]{ibm_p9regs}, the core numbers that the
per-core sensors carry~\cite{smith2020p9layout}, and places the six quads in two bands of three,
each quad's cores stacked in one column (Fig.~\ref{fig:layout}, inset), so index neighbours are die
neighbours only within a quad. What can the thermal data add? A candidate layout's score is the
Pearson correlation, over the 66 slice pairs with equal weight, between its slice distance and the
slices' thermal similarity (the mean of their four cross-slice core-pair correlations); coupling
falls with distance, so a better layout scores more negative.

Over all 1{,}948 sockets the index order scores $-0.791$ (95\,\% CI $[-0.800,-0.778]$, resampling
racks; random-ordering null $-0.003\pm0.123$, $z=-6.41$, rack-bootstrap $[-6.55,-6.24]$) and beats every
grid of unit spacing
(Fig.~\ref{fig:layout}). An exact search over all $12!/2$ one-dimensional orderings (dynamic
programming over the $2^{12}$ prefix sets) finds $[0,\dots,9,11,10]$ at $-0.793$; the index order
ranks second of $239{,}500{,}800$ in each of three months. IBM's layout, at the diagram's
proportions, scores $-0.824$ ($[-0.832,-0.813]$), though that score turns on the diagram's assumed
proportions rather than on sampling: over 90 column, gap and pitch scalings it ranges $-0.846$ to
$-0.311$ and beats the best one-dimensional ordering in 34 of them, and in 2022-02 the two tie
exactly. And it wins only through the six same-quad pairs: over the 60 pairs in different quads the
index order fits better ($-0.814$ against $-0.734$).

\paragraph*{What the coupling follows} The score uses cross-slice pairs only, so shared L2/L3 does
not enter. Within a quad the coupling matches IBM's layout: at index distance~1, neighbours in one
quad couple at $+0.37$ to $+0.48$, neighbours in different quads at $+0.10$. Beyond the quad it
follows the order in which the surviving cores are numbered, not the diagram. Within a die column,
slice $4c{+}1$ couples on average more with its index neighbour $4c{+}2$ at the far die edge than
with the nearer slice $4c{+}3$ across the nest (by $0.055$, CI $[0.046,0.066]$), where heat-source
models on IBM's layout predict the opposite sign; and for the same two slices, coupling falls by
$0.080$ ($[0.073,0.086]$) per active slice between them, against at most $0.032$ in those models.
All three months agree. Only a small same-band excess ($+0.044$, $[0.040,0.049]$) points to the
diagram's two bands. \textbf{The layout therefore comes from IBM's documentation, not from
telemetry: thermal coupling confirms the quads and, beyond them, follows the core numbering,}
plausibly through work placed on consecutive logical CPUs. The spatial reading of
Sec.~\ref{sec:cluster} rests on the documented quads, not on a thermally recovered ordering.


\subsection{A lot boundary at rack 22}
\label{sec:lot}
\paragraph*{What a rack is here} Nodes fill 49 cabinets of 20 with
$\mathrm{id}=20\,\mathrm{rack}+\mathrm{slot}$, and every cabinet's $(X,Y)$ position on the
machine-room floor is documented~\cite{exadata_repo}, so the rack index is a physical coordinate,
not a label. That is why it is informative, and why it must be handled carefully: it conflates the
\emph{hardware population} (parts installed together would fill neighbouring racks) with
\emph{room position} (cooling, airflow, height in cabinet). We separate the two below. We call the
two rack-aligned populations \emph{lots}; procurement batches are their most plausible reading, but
no delivery record was available to confirm it.

A changepoint scan maximises Welch $t$ at rack~22, $t=26.6$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:rack}). At node
granularity the boundary is sharper still: $t=26.8$ between nodes 438 and 439, with the 40 sockets
immediately before at 95.0\,\% slice-0 harvested and the 40 immediately after at 42.5\,\%.
Node~439 sits in the topmost slot of rack~21 yet behaves like the later population (0 of its 2
sockets have slice~0 harvested, against 2 of 2 for each of nodes 430--438), so the
boundary is rack-aligned to within a single node (Table~\ref{tab:lots}).

Lot A marginals fall steeply with slice index; Lot B is nearly flat. Beyond the boundary the rack
carries little: same-rack sockets share more harvested slices than a fleet-wide shuffle predicts
($1.565$ vs $1.453\pm0.008$, $z=+13.3$), but shuffling within each lot removes the excess
($1.559\pm0.007$, $z=+0.9$), and between-rack variance within lots is at most marginal (smallest
$p=0.017$, slice~0, of 12 slices).

\paragraph*{The boundary, tested rather than maximised}
\label{par:changepoint}
A $t$ of 26.6 obtained by scanning every split is a \emph{maximum} over dependent tests, and
cannot be read as a significance level. We therefore calibrate it. Permuting whole racks (which
preserves rack sizes and any within-rack dependence), the null distribution of
$\max|t|$ has mean $7.08\pm2.41$ and never exceeds $17.95$ in 4{,}000 draws, against the observed
$26.6$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:cp}): calibrated $p=2.5\times10^{-4}$, the floor of a 4{,}000-draw test.
Resampling sockets or whole nodes within each rack, with the racks held in place, puts the
breakpoint at rack~22 in 100\,\% and 99.6\,\% of 2{,}000 resamples: a stability check, not a
confidence interval. Under the fitted two-lot model, resampling whole racks within each side
returns rack~22 in 99.7\,\% and a rack-level beta-binomial parametric bootstrap in 98.7\,\%, and
the rack-level likelihood favours rack~22 over rack~23 by a factor of $5.6\times10^{4}$. Leaving
out each rack in turn, \textbf{all 49} fits split the remaining racks where the full data do. The
scan is restricted to boundaries leaving at least 100 sockets per side; without that guard the
argmax is captured by a four-node partial rack at the end of the id range, which we exclude and
report (Sec.~\ref{sec:pops}).

\paragraph*{Two populations, chosen out of sample} Rather than a stopping rule on $|t|$, we segment
the rack-ordered fleet into $k$ contiguous populations by dynamic programming and choose $k$ by
\emph{rack-held-out} likelihood, since in-sample fit always rewards more segments. Under a
twelve-slice Bernoulli score, held-out log-likelihood per socket is $-7.360$ at $k=1$,
\textbf{$-7.133$ at $k=2$}, then $-7.139$, $-7.157$ and $-7.149$ at $k=3,4,5$. Because that score
ignores the four-slice constraint, we repeat the selection with three models on the 495 valid maps
(conditional Bernoulli, the pairwise model of Sec.~\ref{sec:yield}, a Dirichlet-smoothed
multinomial): each selects $k=2$ with the cut at rack~22, except the multinomial at its lightest
smoothing, the weakest predictor of all, which prefers $k=1$. For slice~0, a step also predicts
held-out racks better than a logistic trend in rack index. Among one to five rack-ordered
populations, two is the out-of-sample optimum, and the change is a step rather than a gradient.

\paragraph*{The lot is predictable, not merely detectable} The operationally meaningful question
is whether a harvest map places a rack the model has never seen in the right lot. Holding out each
rack in turn, we re-locate the boundary and fit the two lot models on the other 48 racks only, then
classify the held-out rack's sockets by likelihood ratio: \textbf{72.9\,\% of individual sockets}
are assigned to the lot the full data give them, against a 55.1\,\% majority-class baseline, and
\textbf{47 of 49 held-out racks (95.9\,\%)} by majority vote. Lot membership is thus predictable
out of sample, which a large in-sample $t$ alone would not establish; the lots themselves remain
inferred, not documented deliveries.

\paragraph*{The hardware, not the room} \label{par:geom} Because rack index conflates hardware
population with geometry, we test the geometric alternative directly. Raw correlations with slice-0 status are
substantial for the machine-room row ($r=-0.414$ for $Y$) and visible for aisle position
($r=+0.124$), exactly as expected when both track rack index. \textbf{Once the lot is accounted
for, they vanish}: partial correlations are $+0.017$ for $Y$ ($p=0.44$) and $+0.014$ for $X$
($p=0.53$). Only the slot, meaning height within the cabinet, retains a residual $-0.054$ ($p=0.017$,
0.3\,\% of variance), which is marginal under a three-test correction and would in any case be
consistent with racks being filled in installation order. The boundary also falls inside a row:
in the middle row, Lot~A racks 18--21 have slice~0 harvested on 91.9\,\% of sockets and Lot~B racks
22--32 on 39.1\,\% (rack rates 87.5--95.0\,\% against 27.5--47.5\,\%), and adjacent cabinets 21
and 22 differ 90.0\,\% versus 42.5\,\%. Nor does the signature stay with the rack: dies arriving
after commissioning in replacement-class map changes (Sec.~\ref{sec:taxonomy}) have slice~0
harvested on 33 of 54 in Lot~A racks (61\,\%, node-bootstrap 95\,\% CI $[48,73]$, against Lot~A's
88\,\%) and on 21 of 42 in Lot~B racks, with no detectable difference between the lots ($p=0.30$).
The signature belongs to the parts installed at commissioning, or to how they were set up then,
not to the room or to any setting that stays with the rack. Rack order can still stand for how the
two populations differed when they were racked (delivery, vendor-side provisioning or
installation-time setup), which telemetry alone cannot separate.

\paragraph*{The boundary is not an artefact of the coverage filter} Because every structural
result is computed on \emph{clean} socket-days, a lot-correlated dropout could in principle
manufacture this contrast. It does not. Clean-day rates are near-identical between lots (0.909 vs
0.902), coverage is uncorrelated with the harvest pattern (corr with slice-0 status $=+0.004$,
$p=0.88$), and restricting to the 1{,}847 sockets with $\ge$90\,\% clean-day coverage leaves the
contrast unchanged at $+49.0$ percentage points versus $+48.9$ over all sockets.

\subsection{Configuration lifetime}
\label{sec:lifecycle}
POWER9 firmware \emph{could} also alter the active set after manufacture: a core with a detected
hardware error can be recorded in a persistent GARD record that hostboot applies at every IPL and
\texttt{opal-gard} can clear, and a core-count override can switch cores off for
licensing~\cite{ibm_p9um,hostboot_p9,ibm_deconfig,skiboot_gard}. Whether harvest maps are static in practice is therefore an empirical question, which we answer by
aggregating every core sample to (node, socket, day) counts across the dataset.
All numbers in this section are over the complete 31-month record (2020-03 to 2022-09).

\paragraph*{The count is very nearly invariant} Across all 31 months (1{,}671{,}297 socket-days
over 858 reporting days and 984 nodes), \textbf{99.9847\% report exactly 16 active cores}. Of the 256
exceptions, 39 have an odd count, which no baseline map produces: 11 fall on days the OS
confirms a single-core deconfiguration (below), 4 on one socket before OS collection began, 21
are one sensor lost while the OS counted all 128 CPUs, so the collector can also drop a core, and
the remaining three are two unions spanning a reconfiguration and one socket with no earlier clean
map. The 209 counts above 16 are all partially sampled days spanning a
reconfiguration.\footnote{The three groups quoted here are not a partition of the 256: two
odd-count socket-days are also above 16 (17 and 23 active cores), and the tenth 14-core day is not
fully sampled, so 39, 209 and nine cover 255 of them.} Only \textbf{nine} socket-days are
fully-sampled with an even count below 16, and these are the subject of the guard analysis below.

\paragraph*{Configurations are near-static} Restricting to \emph{clean} socket-days (16 cores, each
at $>$90\% of the 4{,}320 samples expected at 20\,s cadence) leaves 1{,}517{,}473 socket-days, a
median of 775 observed days per socket. \textbf{89.5\% of sockets never change configuration} (95\,\% CI $[88.1,90.8]$)
(1{,}756 of 1{,}962); 183 have two distinct sets, 19 have three, three have four and one has five.

\paragraph*{Core-guard episodes} The nine anomalous socket-days resolve into
\textbf{three sustained episodes on three distinct sockets}, the episodes that meet this strict
criterion (Table~\ref{tab:guard}). In every case the socket loses \emph{exactly one slice}, a
complete $(2k,2k{+}1)$ pair, and runs on 14 cores for 2--4 consecutive fully-sampled days, a loss
with the signature of a GARD record applied at IPL (the record itself is not in the telemetry). In
the commissioning-era case the slice later returns; in both steady-state cases the socket instead
returns with a different map (Table~\ref{tab:guard}).

Rates need care. One of the three episodes (347\,p1, 2020-03-13--16) falls inside the
commissioning window identified and excluded later in this section, so the strict criterion gives
\textbf{two steady-state episodes}. An episode counts only on fully-sampled days, so the
denominator is \emph{observed} exposure, 3{,}991 socket-years over the steady state:
\textbf{5.0 strict episodes per 10{,}000 socket-years}, exact Poisson 95\,\% CI $[0.6,18.1]$, a
factor of thirty wide. Counting every deconfiguration the OS confirms, slice or single core
(below), gives 26 $[12,49]$ per 10{,}000 over 3{,}500 OS-observed socket-years. Both are
order-of-magnitude statements, and durations are lower bounds.

\emph{Independently confirmed by the operating system.} Of 990 nodes, exactly \textbf{three} ever
report \textbf{120} logical CPUs, which is $128-8$, precisely one slice of two cores at four
threads. Those three nodes are \textbf{347, 411 and 946}: the same three, and only those, on which
the strict criterion finds a slice episode. Under a null in which the OS flagged three arbitrary nodes, the
chance of matching our three is $1/\binom{990}{3}=6.2\times10^{-9}$.

The dates agree too, and agree in the direction the method predicts. For node~411 the OS window is
2021-11-12--16 against a BMC episode of 11-13--15; for node~946, 2020-08-30--09-02 against
08-31--09-01. In both cases the OS window \emph{brackets} the BMC one, exactly as it should, since
a BMC episode is counted only on fully-sampled days and its duration is therefore a lower bound.
Node~347's OS deviation is a recurrence: in 2020-10 the BMC loses the same slice again, on days
too partially sampled for our strict criterion.

Three events recovered from thermal-sensor presence
alone are confirmed, node-for-node, by the operating system's own CPU count, a quantity produced
by a different collector, from a different layer of the stack, that never touches a BMC sensor.

\emph{Every OS departure from 128 is seen by the BMC.} Six further nodes report \textbf{124}
logical CPUs ($128-4$, one SMT4 \emph{core}), and on each the BMC loses exactly one core sensor
from the same boot (whole days on five, from mid-day on node~145), a single-core deconfiguration
our slice-granular criterion excludes. All \textbf{nine} OS departures are thus matched node for
node and day for day, and each is followed within six days by a new map on that socket that no
other socket gave up that week, as a failing processor replaced would produce
(Sec.~\ref{sec:taxonomy}).

\emph{Not an artefact of a tight filter.} The criterion above is strict, so we re-ran the search
against the \emph{adjacent observed day} rather than the modal map, requiring only that surviving
slices exceed 50\,\% sampling rather than 90\,\%. It returns \textbf{the same three episodes and
no others}, so the three are not an artefact of the threshold. By construction the criterion cannot
see single-core losses or losses that run into a reconfiguration day; the OS counts those above.

\emph{Ruling out a collector fault.} The competing explanation is that the collector dropped
exactly those two metric streams. We test it directly: for each episode we ask whether the
\emph{other} sensors on the same node, in particular on the same socket, kept reporting. They
did. In all three episodes the two slice sensors read zero for the entire window while
\textbf{all ten control metrics, including \texttt{pX\_power} and \texttt{pX\_vdd\_temp} on the
same socket, sustained a full 4{,}320 samples per day} (Fig.~\ref{fig:guard}). A collection fault
that silences precisely the two cores of one slice for several consecutive days, while leaving
every other sensor on the same socket at full cadence, is not a plausible account.

\emph{And they sit across reboots, as the mechanism requires.} A GARD record is applied by
hostboot at IPL, so a deconfiguration, and its later clearing, should each land on a boot. On the
independent \texttt{boottime} signal of Sec.~\ref{par:reboot}, node~946's episode of
2020-08-31--09-01 falls between boots on 08-30 and 09-03, node~411's of 2021-11-13--15 between
boots on 11-12 and 11-15/16, and all nine OS-confirmed deconfigurations begin on a boot day.
Node~347's March episode predates \texttt{boottime} collection (2020-05-05): absent coverage, not
an absent reboot.

We therefore attribute these episodes to firmware deconfiguration with the signature of a GARD
record applied at boot; the record itself was not read. We are not aware of an earlier report of
such episodes recovered from fleet telemetry.

\paragraph*{Changes never occur during operation} Of \textbf{235} transitions across the full

\paragraph*{A reporting gap is not a reboot, so we measure the reboot}
\label{par:reboot}
That argument infers a reboot from a \emph{gap}, and collectors stop for many reasons. The
inference is unnecessary: the Ganglia plugin publishes \texttt{boottime}, the epoch at which a
node last booted, so a reboot is directly observable as a change in that value. Extracting it for
all 31 months gives 668{,}524 node-days across 990 nodes, of which 1.74\,\% carry a reboot.

Of the \textbf{132 steady-state transitions with boot coverage, 126 (95.5\,\%) coincide with an
observed reboot}, against \textbf{47.6\,\%} expected once control intervals are matched on
length ($49.0\pm4.6$ of 103 node-level transitions; the raw per-node-day rate is 1.74\,\%, but a
transition sits across a multi-day reporting gap, so it is not the comparator). The length-matched
comparison is Table~\ref{tab:reboot}, and the result survives the dependence between intervals:
permuting transition labels only \emph{within} node $\times$ interval-length strata, with a node's
two sockets as one unit, gives 99 of 103 node-level transitions with a reboot against $49.0\pm4.6$
expected (exact conditional $p=6\times10^{-31}$), a length-standardised excess of $+63$ points
(node-cluster bootstrap 95\,\% CI $[+57,+68]$).

The claim is therefore no longer that changes coincide with missing data, but that they coincide

transitions contain a boot and none is observed without one}; three lack any bracketing
\texttt{boottime} observation.

The only two apparent counterexamples confirm the rule once examined at full timestamp resolution.
Nodes~315 (p1) and~342 (p0) appear to change within a single day, producing 20- and 22-core
socket-days. In fact both cut over on 2021-03-02 across a \emph{sub-day reporting
gap}: on node~315 the outgoing cores $\{4,5,20,21\}$ report until 08:42 and the incoming cores
$\{8,9,10,11\}$ resume at 09:10, a 28-minute outage, with exactly 16 cores before and 16 after; the
20/22-core counts are unions across the cutover. Node~342 cuts over in the same window, and
\texttt{boottime} records a reboot of both nodes that morning (09:10 and 09:13).

\paragraph*{Staticity as a prediction, not a description} The figures above describe the record
in-sample. We also test them out-of-sample: derive each socket's map from the \emph{first} half of
the record (2020-03 to 2021-06) and use it to predict every clean socket-day in the \emph{second}
(2021-07 to 2022-09). Over \textbf{839{,}067 held-out socket-days the prediction is correct
96.77\,\% of the time} (95\,\% CI $[96.73,96.81]$), and the errors are not diffuse: they fall on \textbf{89 of 1{,}962
sockets} (4.5\,\%), which are the sockets that genuinely reconfigure between the two halves. A map
measured once therefore forecasts a socket more than a year later.

\paragraph*{Changes concentrate at commissioning} The 235 transitions involve 132 nodes on 81
distinct dates, but 2020-03-20 alone accounts for 63 and the top three dates for 41\,\%
(Fig.~\ref{fig:trans}), an early, synchronised regime that the next paragraph locates and excludes.

\paragraph*{Commissioning period, identified and excluded}
\label{par:commissioning}
Two independent signals locate the end of commissioning without assuming a date. Telemetry
coverage, the fraction of socket-days that are clean, is 0.82, 0.00 and 0.40 in 2020-03, -04 and
-05, then 0.72--1.00 in every later month but one (2021-03, 0.50). The transition rate falls from
91 in 2020-03 to a median of 4 per month thereafter, and the operator announced Marconi100's
postponed start of production for 2020-05-04~\cite{cineca_m100_status}. We therefore treat
2020-03 to 2020-05 as commissioning and re-run every result on 2020-06 onward:

\begin{itemize}\itemsep1pt
\item \textbf{The harvest maps are unchanged}: for all 1{,}960 sockets with a map in both windows,
      the recovered map is \emph{byte-identical} (100.0\,\%). Marginals agree to 0.1 percentage
      points and the pattern count is 443 either way.
\item \textbf{The lot boundary is unchanged}: Lot A 88.2\,\%, Lot B 39.2\,\%, Welch $t=26.6$.
\item \textbf{Zero transitions during operation still holds}: of the 136 steady-state transitions,
      \emph{none} occurs between consecutive reporting days.
\item \textbf{The fleet-wide clustering disappears with the commissioning window}: the largest
      single-date cluster in steady state is 11 events (8\,\% of steady-state transitions), against
      63 (27\,\%) on 2020-03-20.
\end{itemize}

Every structural and longitudinal claim in this paper therefore rests on the post-commissioning
period, and none of them depends on the anomalous early months.

\paragraph*{Some apparent changes are socket relabelling} Socket relabelling
(\textsc{relabel}, Sec.~\ref{sec:taxonomy}) accounts for \textbf{19\%} of the 105 steady-state node-level map-change events (20; 95\,\%
CI $[12,28]$) and 20\% of the 230 socket-level transitions across the whole record where both
sockets are observable.

\paragraph*{Caveat} An apparent change can arise from (a)~genuine hardware reconfiguration,
(b)~socket relabelling, or (c)~a change in the BMC sensor-ID mapping, e.g.\ after a firmware
update. Telemetry separates these only in part: a fleet-wide remap would impose one permutation of
slice indices on every socket it touches, and neither the March 2020 cluster nor any steady-state
date with four or more changes admits one (Sec.~\ref{sec:taxonomy}). The March cluster also lies
in the commissioning period excluded above, and every result is reported on the steady-state
period. The ambiguity is therefore bounded rather than load-bearing.


\subsection{A taxonomy of map changes}
\label{sec:taxonomy}
A harvest map is carried by the die in a socket, so a change in the map is informative about what
physically happened. A map is a signature, not a serial number, so we name each class of change by
the event it is \emph{consistent with} (Fig.~\ref{fig:hier}, bottom). If p0 loses exactly the slices
p1 gains and vice versa, the same two dies are present and only the tags moved (\textsc{relabel}).
If exactly one socket's map changes while the other is observed and unchanged across the same
interval, the socket received another processor (\textsc{cpu-swap}); if both change and it is not a
mirror, both did (\textsc{node-swap}). A count dropping below 16 at a boot by one slice or one core
is a deconfiguration with no die movement (\textsc{guard}, Sec.~\ref{sec:lifecycle}).

The split (Table~\ref{tab:taxonomy}) validates the taxonomy on its own terms. During commissioning \textsc{node-swap}
dominates (40 of 56, 71\,\%), since whole machines are being installed and reconfigured. In steady state
the picture inverts: \textsc{cpu-swap} (with \textsc{cpu-swap?}) accounts for 70\,\% of the 105
steady-state node-level events and \textsc{node-swap} for 11\,\%, which is what a maintenance regime
that services individual processors looks like. Steady
state contains 105 events on 87 distinct nodes over 28 months, about 3.8 per month.

Two consequences are worth stating. First, \textbf{19\,\% of steady-state map changes move no
die}; a study that read every change as a replacement would overcount by that much. Second,
counting one die per \textsc{cpu-swap} and two per \textsc{node-swap} gives \textbf{96 die
changes} over 4{,}561 socket-years of calendar exposure (1{,}960 sockets over the 850-day
post-commissioning window; a change is recovered by comparing maps \emph{across} a reporting gap,
so an unobserved day is still a day at risk): sockets \textbf{change die at 2.1\,\% per year},
95\,\% CI $[1.6,2.7]$ by rack-cluster bootstrap. The exact Poisson $[1.7,2.6]$ is too narrow,
since a \textsc{node-swap} moves two dies and changes cluster by rack (37 of 49 racks, $z=+2.55$,
$p=0.016$); observed clean socket-days as denominator give 2.4\,\%. Not every die change is a new
processor. Because a map travels with its die, we \emph{trace} a die when a socket's new map is
exactly the old map of another socket whose map changed within seven days, one maintenance
window. \textbf{50 of the 96} are traced (4.4 expected by chance; 42 of them on the same day): 43
come from another node, 36 of them in 18 two-way exchanges, and 7 from the partner socket. The
other 46, new processors at an inferred \textbf{1.0\,\% per socket-year} (rack-cluster CI
$[0.7,1.4]$; 1.2\,\% if only same-day moves are traced), include all nine changes that follow an
OS-confirmed deconfiguration. Other
misreadings are small: a replacement die with the old map escapes with probability 2.1\,\%
(Lot~A) or 0.26\,\% (Lot~B), and the data fit neither a firmware remap nor in-place
reconfiguration. Only service records could measure each class's precision.

\subsection{A clustering parameter, fitted per lot}
\label{sec:yield}
Section~\ref{sec:cluster} establishes clustering against a null; it does not \emph{quantify} it,
and the yield models we invoke~\cite{koren1993negbinom,bae2007waferyield} are parameterised by a
clustering strength. Those models describe defect \emph{counts} per die, which we cannot observe:
the SKU fixes the count at exactly four harvested slices. What is observable is the conditional
spatial arrangement given four, so we fit the natural conditional analogue, a pairwise-interaction
model on the twelve slice positions: for a harvested set $S\subset\{0,\dots,11\}$ with $|S|=4$,
\[
  P(S)\;\propto\;\exp\Big(\textstyle\sum_{k\in S}\beta_k \;+\; \gamma\,\mathrm{adj}(S)\Big),
\]
where $\mathrm{adj}(S)=|\{k: k\in S,\ k{+}1\in S\}|$ counts index-adjacent pairs. The $\beta_k$
absorb per-slice propensity (the slice-0 asymmetry) and $\gamma$ measures adjacency \emph{beyond}
what those propensities explain; since $|S|=4$ for every $S$, a constant added to all $\beta_k$
cancels, so we impose $\sum_k\beta_k=0$. The normalising constant is an exact sum over all
$\binom{12}{4}=495$ patterns, so the log-likelihood is exact and strictly concave, and Newton's
method converges to a gradient norm below $10^{-8}$.

Three things follow (Table~\ref{tab:yield}). First, \textbf{residual adjacency is significant in
both lots} ($\chi^2_1$ of
174 and 106, $p\ll0.001$; rack-clustered Wald $z$ of $+15.9$ and $+7.5$): mean adjacent-pair counts are 1.463 and 1.218 observed against 1.203 and 0.993 under
the no-clustering model. Second, \textbf{the lots differ significantly in clustering strength}, not only in their
marginals. Here the uncertainty needs care: an i.i.d.\ likelihood over sockets gives
$z=+4.47$, but sockets are \emph{not} independent: each node contributes two, and sockets in a
rack may share more than their lot (Sec.~\ref{sec:lot} finds at most a marginal excess), so
resampling racks is the conservative choice. Resampling \emph{racks} rather than sockets changes
the standard errors by $0.85\times$ to $1.47\times$ and puts the difference at
$\gamma_A-\gamma_B = +0.309$, $z=+4.16$, 95\,\% CI $[+0.168,+0.455]$; a delete-one-rack jackknife
($z=+4.04$) and a rack-clustered sandwich ($z=+4.06$) agree. The claim survives every uncertainty
model we tried; we report the clustered figure. Third, and most usefully, the two effects
\emph{separate}: Lot~A's $\beta_0=+2.51$ dwarfs every other propensity while Lot~B's $\beta$ vector
is nearly flat (range $+0.34$ to $-0.13$), yet both carry a substantial positive $\gamma$. The
slice-0 asymmetry and the residual adjacency are therefore statistically separable summaries of
the maps rather than two views of one.

\paragraph*{The model is useful and incomplete, and we show both}
\label{par:ppc}
A likelihood-ratio test tells us $\gamma\ne0$; it does not tell us the model is adequate. We
therefore draw 10{,}000 replicate data sets from the fitted model, refit each (a parametric
bootstrap, not a posterior-predictive check), and compare them to the data on quantities it was
\emph{not} fitted to. The model is fitted to the per-slice counts and the adjacency total, so
the full pattern distribution, the number of distinct patterns, and the largest gap between
harvested slices are all out-of-statistic checks.

It passes some and fails others. The count of distinct patterns is reproduced (443 observed
against $436.4\pm6.3$ replicated, $p=0.16$). But the pattern-frequency discrepancy is not: 955.6
observed against $481.5\pm31.1$ replicated (none as large, $p=1/(B+1)=10^{-4}$), and the observed
maps are systematically \emph{more compact} than the model predicts (mean maximum gap 4.469
against $4.685\pm0.044$, $p=10^{-4}$). The residual co-harvest matrix (Fig.~\ref{fig:ppc}) shows exactly
where it fails, and the failure is structured: because $\gamma$ is fitted to the \emph{total}
adjacency, the model reproduces that total by construction but distributes it wrongly across the
die, over-predicting co-harvest at slices $(7,8)$, $(3,4)$ and $(5,6)$ (simulation-calibrated
$z=-7.2$, $-5.7$, $-5.0$) and under-predicting it at $(0,1)$, $(0,2)$ and $(7,10)$ ($+9.2$, $+6.1$,
$+5.0$); 10 of 66 cells lie outside the simultaneous 95\,\% band, and the $(7,8)$ miss recurs in
each lot fitted alone. \textbf{A single, position-independent adjacency term does not capture the
spatial structure}, and the miss is regular: adjacency within a quad (slices $2m,2m{+}1$) carries
$\gamma=+0.92\pm0.04$, across quads only $+0.28\pm0.06$ (difference $z=+13.9$, racks resampled),
and even that split leaves misfit. The term still captures a large
part of it (the same discrepancy against the no-clustering model is 1631.9), so $\gamma$
summarises a real effect rather than an artefact. We report the misspecification because a reader
otherwise has no way to tell a fitted parameter from an adequate model.

\textbf{The clean-day threshold is inert.} Moving it from 0.50 to 0.99 discards 18\,\% of
socket-days and changes nothing that matters: the same 1{,}962 sockets (1{,}960 at 0.99), 442--443
patterns, an identical 61.2\,\% slice-0 rate and an identical lot contrast. This is a stronger
statement than ``we chose 0.90''.

instead of the mode changes 163 of 1{,}962 maps (8.3\,\%) and taking the last changes 49

change under ``first'' than ``last'') reflects that commissioning-era configurations are the ones
later replaced. The lot contrast ranges only over $t=25.9$--$28.4$. The \emph{strict} rule, which

resolution.

\subsection{The map and socket power: bounded rather than dismissed}
\label{sec:impact}
Recovering a hidden hardware property is only useful if it changes something. We therefore ask
whether the harvest map predicts socket behaviour, using a \textbf{within-node paired contrast}:
the two sockets of a node share chassis, airflow, inlet air and power supply, so the difference
$p_0-p_1$ removes the node-level confounds that make cross-node power comparisons unreliable. We
summarise each socket's map by the mean index of its active slices, the number of adjacent active
pairs, and the count of active slices in the low half, and regress the socket-to-socket difference
in mean CPU power on the difference in each summary, one summary per model
(Fig.~\ref{fig:impact}). Each node-day carries the map in force that day, from the run of
identical clean days containing it, rather than the socket's most common map over the whole
record.

Pairing does not remove a workload the scheduler places unevenly between the sockets, and the
node-level covariates we adjust for (utilisation, load average, inlet air, month) cannot measure it.
The map, however, is fixed at boot and invisible to the scheduler, which allocates 16
interchangeable cores per socket, so uneven placement biases the slope only through its
correlation with the map difference. Where the job table records each job's cores (June--October
2020), placement explains 40\,\% of the variance of $p_0-p_1$ but correlates with the map
difference at only $|r|\le0.021$; adjusting for it moves each estimate about 1\,W away from
zero, and no interval extends beyond $5.0$\,W. Later job records carry no core layout, so the
full record cannot be adjusted the same way. Idle node-days, with almost no workload to place,
give a second check.

\paragraph*{Failing to reject is not the same as showing negligible} An earlier version of this
analysis used six months of power data, reported $r=+0.039$ (permutation $p=0.003$) explaining
0.16\,\% of variance, and concluded that harvest maps are ``operationally invisible''. That
conclusion does not survive two corrections, and we state the revision plainly because it changes
a headline claim.

The first correction is the instrument. ``No measurable effect'' cannot be established by a small
$r$; the question is whether the effect is small \emph{enough}, which requires an equivalence
test against a margin fixed in advance. We adopt $\pm5$\,W per socket, $7.2$\,\% of the
$69.0$\,W mean draw and well inside the power spread of up to 23\,\% measured between nominally
identical processors~\cite{inadomi2015variation}. Any margin is a judgement, so
Table~\ref{tab:tost} also gives the smallest margin each interval clears, which settles the verdict
at $\pm2$ and $\pm10$\,W as well. The second correction is
the uncertainty: node-days within a rack are not independent, and the previous interval treated
them as if they were.

contemporaneous maps and rack-clustered bootstrap intervals, the result is
(Table~\ref{tab:tost}):

\textbf{Two of the three summaries are equivalent at $\pm5$\,W, all three at $\pm10$\,W.}

low-half count correlate at $-0.89$); over the map pairs that do occur, the same joint fit spans

nodes whose maps never change, which excludes every relabel and map-changing replacement, give
mean index $[-0.1,+6.5]$\,W, and on idle node-days adjacency gives $-5.0$\,W, $[-7.2,-0.5]$, the one design
in which an adjusted interval excludes zero; it weights nodes by idle time, and with one
observation per node-epoch it is $-2.5$\,W, $[-5.1,+0.1]$. The summary is therefore
\emph{bounded and small, but not provably negligible}: a few watts between the most dissimilar
maps, below the spread between sockets, yet not inside $\pm5$\,W for every summary in every
design. The claim that the map is operationally invisible stays withdrawn. Note also that this does not weaken
the provenance argument of Sec.~\ref{sec:lot}: the lot signature travels with the dies, not
with the rack they sit in.

\subsection{The map does not usefully predict which dies get replaced}
\label{sec:reliability}
The map is set before a die enters service; replacement is what the field eventually
concluded about it. If dies whose harvested slices sit in a tight cluster were the ones that
failed, that would tie manufacturing variability to operational reliability far more directly
than any power correlation. We test it.

The design is a discrete-time survival model in calendar months (June 2020 to September 2022; all
but two dies enter in the first month, so this is also time since entry). A die is at risk from
its first steady-state clean day until its event; every other die is followed to the end of the
record, so censoring is purely administrative. The event, inferred from the map rather than read
from a service record, is the first month in which the die's map leaves its node: a
\textsc{cpu-swap} or \textsc{node-swap} in which the map is not re-found on the partner socket.
Dies are followed by their map, so a \textsc{relabel} moves no die, and two dies leaving together
are two events. The interval hazard $h_i(t)=P(T_i{=}t\mid T_i{\ge}t)$ is
$1-\exp\{-\exp(\alpha_t+x_i^{\top}\beta)\}$, the grouped proportional-hazards
model~\cite{prentice1978grouped}, so $e^{\beta}$ is a hazard ratio; $\alpha_t$ is one baseline
effect per month, shrunk towards their mean, as monthly events range from 0 to 15. Covariates
come from the die's \emph{first} steady-state map: slice-0 status, the number of index-adjacent
harvested pairs, the dispersion of the harvested indices, the rarity of the pattern ($-\log$ of
its smoothed frequency) and the lot. This gives \textbf{85 events over 53{,}716 die-months}, a
monthly hazard of 0.158\,\%; 44 of these dies were never seen again and 41 reappeared on another
node, traced as in Sec.~\ref{sec:taxonomy}. (The 96 of Sec.~\ref{sec:taxonomy} also counts 7 dies
re-found on the partner socket and 4 later departures of dies already counted or installed during
the record.)

\textbf{No feature is a significant predictor.} No hazard ratio's rack-clustered bootstrap interval
excludes 1: 1.25 $[0.51,3.26]$ for slice-0 status and 0.64 $[0.33,1.24]$ for lot~B (both $0{\to}1$),
and per standard deviation 0.75 $[0.51,1.05]$ for adjacent harvested pairs, 0.85 $[0.63,1.12]$ for
dispersion and 0.85 $[0.56,1.21]$ for rarity, point estimates unchanged in the second decimal
under a constant or spline baseline. Under seven-fold cross-validation grouped by rack and repeated over ten
partitions, with rarity and scaling learned in the training folds, the risk-set concordance (each
month's events against the dies of the same held-out fold still at risk that
month~\cite{heagerty2005survival}) is \textbf{0.55} (95\,\% CI $[0.48,0.61]$) against a
rack-permutation null of $0.50\pm0.04$ ($p=0.12$; Fig.~\ref{fig:nulls}, left). Adding the map does
not improve the held-out survival log-likelihood over the monthly baseline alone ($-2.1$ nats,
$p=0.14$), nor the integrated Brier score.

We report this as a bounded null rather than an absence of evidence~[OI-16], and the bounds are
asymmetric: the intervals exclude a hazard increase above 21\,\% per standard deviation of any
continuous feature, but not a protective effect down to about 0.5, and for the binary slice-0 and
lot contrasts they exclude only effects beyond about threefold. What the data do exclude is useful
prediction of these events from these features: even the upper confidence bound on concordance,
0.61, lies below the 0.7 conventionally required for acceptable
discrimination~\cite{hosmer2013applied}, and it stays below 0.7 for dies never seen again (0.66), for
\textsc{cpu-swap}-like events alone (0.59) and for the broadest endpoint, which also counts
relabelling (0.64; its nominal rack-permutation $p=0.015$ does not survive correction for six
endpoints, \texttt{LIMITATIONS.md}); \textsc{node-swap}-like events alone (13) are too few to bound. Two readings are consistent with it: whatever set the disabled positions
is unrelated to what kills a part in the field, or replacement is dominated by causes
unrelated to the die. Either way, an operator should not use the harvest map as a
reliability covariate, which, with Sec.~\ref{sec:impact}, completes a consistent picture of a
property that is highly informative about \emph{provenance} and at most weakly informative about
\emph{behaviour}.

\subsection{How much telemetry does the disclosure cost, and what closes it?}
\label{sec:channel}
We have shown the maps are recoverable; an operator publishing such a dataset needs to know how
cheaply, and which mitigations would work. Both follow from one asymmetry: a harvested core can
never emit a sample, so the channel has \emph{no false positives}. Recovery fails only if an
active core stays silent for the whole window, so the question is not how long one must watch but
how many samples per core survive collection.

Under healthy collection that question is almost vacuous. The median core reports
\textbf{4{,}320 of 4{,}320} expected samples per day, so it appears in essentially every 20\,s
slot. Measured on the raw timestamps of 2022-08, a \textbf{single sampling interval recovers the
exact map in 99.95\,\% of socket-intervals}, and in all but $5\times10^{-7}$ of those in which the
socket reports at all.
From the daily counts, again without any model, exact recovery holds for 99.9\,\% of sockets at
\emph{every} window from one day to the full month. Modelling the degraded regime, where the
number of samples surviving from each core is Poisson with mean $\lambda$, independently across
cores, exact recovery is $(1-e^{-\lambda})^{16}$: negligible
at $\lambda\le1$, 0.44 at $\lambda=3$, 0.90 at $\lambda=5$ and 0.99 at $\lambda=7$. The knee is
around five to seven \emph{surviving} samples per core, and half that for an attacker who uses
slice granularity: one surviving sample per active slice suffices (the \emph{slice rule}).

That immediately rules out the obvious defences. Publishing at a coarser cadence does not work:
keeping one 20\,s slot in every six hours still recovers every map from a single day, and one slot
per day 99.95\,\%, because a single sample names its
core just as well as 4{,}320 do. Random deletion does not work either until it becomes
destructive, because maps are static and an attacker pools days. Deleting each sample independently
with probability 99.9\,\% leaves one-day exact recovery at 0.81 (0.9985 under the slice rule); at
99.99\,\% a month still recovers 0.99997 (Fig.~\ref{fig:nulls}, right), and holding one-month
recovery below one half takes 99.998\,\% deletion, or 99.9991\,\% against the slice rule. \textbf{Reducing resolution is not a mitigation, because the channel is carried by
which sensors \emph{exist}, not by what they report.} Only defences that remove the per-core
position work: publishing socket-level aggregates, or renumbering survivors compactly to
$0\dots15$, which is precisely what this BMC does not do. Per-node random renumbering destroys the
map while still leaking the count, but must be redrawn for every release: a fixed permutation turns
the reporting set into a fingerprint unique for 99.7\,\% of sockets, against 4.9\,\% for the maps
themselves.

\section{Discussion}
\paragraph*{Why is slice 0 special?} The single most striking number is that slice~0 is harvested on
88.2\% of Lot~A sockets but only 39.2\% of Lot~B. Two explanations are live. Under
\emph{defect-driven} harvesting, disablement follows wherever killer defects land. Under
\emph{policy-driven} harvesting, slice~0 abuts a shared structure and is preferentially disabled for
reasons of yield policy, binning recipe or physical design; an 88\% rate on one specific slice is
very hard to obtain from random defects. Intel's Xeon Phi has four such favoured positions, read
as a default set disabled on otherwise good dies~\cite{mccalpin2021knl}. A factory core-count
override is not the explanation either: hostboot keeps the lowest-numbered core
pairs~\cite{hostboot_p9}, so, if the BMC index follows firmware numbering, an override would spare
slice~0 and fall on slices 8--11, yet in both lots slice~0 is harvested most often and slices
8--11 are not conspicuously above average (Lot~A 20--25\,\%, Lot~B 31--35\,\% against 33.3\,\%).

The fit of Sec.~\ref{sec:yield} shows that \emph{two statistically distinct effects are present},
and estimates them separately. The propensity vector $\beta$ carries the slice-0 asymmetry and
differs enormously between lots ($\beta_0=+2.51$ versus a flat Lot~B); the interaction parameter
$\gamma$ carries residual spatial association and is significantly positive in \emph{both} lots.
The most economical reading is a change of binning recipe or die source between the two
lots, superimposed on a spatial process present in both.

We are careful about what this does \emph{not} establish. The decomposition is descriptive, not
causal: a positive $\gamma$ says harvested slices are spatially associated beyond what the
per-slice propensities explain, and a correlated \emph{defect} process and a binning \emph{policy}
that disables contiguous blocks both predict exactly that. The model cannot tell them apart, and
neither can any amount of data from one machine. Nor is the model adequate on its own terms
(Sec.~\ref{par:ppc}): it under-predicts how compact the observed maps are, so even the split
between ``propensity'' and ``clustering'' is a summary of the structure rather than a complete
account of it. What survives is that the two lots
differ in \emph{both} parameters, which is a statement about the populations, not about the
mechanism.

\paragraph*{What the rack stands for} The rack-position objection is tested directly in
Sec.~\ref{par:geom}; what rack order can still stand for is the parts' history up to installation,
which is the provenance signal we claim and which telemetry cannot decompose further.

\paragraph*{How identifying is a harvest map?} Since the map is a per-die property, it is fair to
ask whether it identifies a socket, which would raise a different set of questions from the
provenance ones we pursue. Across a whole fleet, one map does not. Over 1{,}962 sockets the
pattern distribution carries $H=8.08$ bits (plug-in estimate, log base 2), or 8.24 and 8.33 bits
after Miller--Madow and Chao--Shen bias correction (rack-jackknife SE 0.12), an effective
$2^{H}=270$--$321$ distinct maps against 495 possible; only \textbf{4.9\,\% of sockets (97) have a
map unique in the fleet}, and a typical map is shared by 4.4 sockets. Uniqueness grows quickly with
side information, though: to 12.1\,\% with the lot, 78.4\,\% with the rack, and a node's two maps
together are unique for 93.0\,\% of nodes. A single map is thus a \emph{partial} identifier, strong
evidence about which population a part came from; a node's pair of maps is a quasi-identifier, which
matters to anyone publishing such telemetry under pseudonymised node names. It records what was
disabled rather than an identity designed into the die, which is why this work sits apart from the
fingerprinting literature~\cite{jetta2025provenance} rather than extending it.

\paragraph*{Operational uses} Fleet telemetry can detect latent hardware cohorts without vendor
cooperation or an asset or procurement database, testing whether the installed parts are one
population and, per Sec.~\ref{par:changepoint}, placing 96\,\% of held-out racks in the right lot.
It can detect field deconfiguration events that would otherwise be visible only in service logs,
and tell dies moved between nodes from new ones. And it \emph{bounds} how much a hardware property
matters: the map does not usefully predict replacement (Sec.~\ref{sec:reliability}), and its
association with socket power is bounded to a few watts (Sec.~\ref{sec:impact}). Bounding an effect is a weaker claim than dismissing one, and
the right one to make here.

\section{Related work}
\paragraph*{Yield, salvage and segmentation} Yield models treat defect statistics directly: the
negative-binomial family~\cite{koren1993negbinom} and spatial wafer-map
models~\cite{bae2007waferyield} describe how many killer defects a die carries and how they
cluster, and are fitted to fab data unavailable to system owners. Disabling a faulty core so that
the die still ships is the baseline response in multicore design~\cite{shivakumar2003redundancy,powell2009salvaging},
and vendors also disable working units to create cheaper products~\cite{deneckere1996damaged,mccalpin2021knl}. We
observe neither the defects nor the reasons, only which positions end up disabled, and only
conditionally on the SKU's fixed count of four.

\paragraph*{Variability in HPC} Manufacturing variability in HPC has been studied through its
\emph{performance} consequences: frequency and power spread under a power
cap~\cite{inadomi2015variation}, turbo-boost variation across nominally identical
processors~\cite{acun2016variation}, and accelerator-level variability in GPU-rich
systems~\cite{sinha2022gpuvariability}; field-reliability studies track components by serial
number~\cite{ostrouchov2020titan}. That literature treats the die as a black box characterised by
its behaviour, or relies on an asset inventory; we recover internal structure from telemetry alone.

\paragraph*{Provenance and disabled-unit maps} Physical unclonable functions and inherent
hardware identifiers exploit analog manufacturing variation to answer \emph{which die is this},
for traceability and counterfeit detection~\cite{jetta2025provenance}. That is a different
question: we do not identify a die, we recover \emph{what was disabled on it}, without
designed-in circuitry or physical access. Closest to us is McCalpin: running a user-mode
instruction on all 4{,}200 Xeon Phi processors of Stampede2, he recovered each die's disabled core
pairs, separated a default disable set from defects, found that the defect rate differed between
the machine's two deployments and counted processor replacements between two
snapshots~\cite{mccalpin2021knl}; on its Xeons he read disabled tiles from a privileged
register~\cite{mccalpin2021capid}. Smith read the enabled cores of one POWER9 machine from the same
BMC sensors we use~\cite{smith2020p9layout}. Our source is public out-of-band telemetry, with no
access to the machines, followed daily for 31 months, and we locate the cohort boundary from the
maps rather than from deployment records. Node-level anomaly and outlier detection in
HPC~\cite{nodesentry2025,badnodes2020} likewise establishes \emph{that} nodes differ without
recovering the hardware structure responsible.

\paragraph*{Telemetry side channels} Temperature sensors are established covert and side
channels: their readings carry data between cores~\cite{masti2015thermal} and leak a kernel's
address-space layout~\cite{kim2022thermalbleed}. Those channels live in sensor \emph{values}; the
one here is carried by which sensors \emph{exist}. The M100 ExaData release itself has an active
downstream literature spanning telemetry foundation models, job power data and operational-data
ontologies~\cite{m100_setdiff,antici2023pm100,m100_ontology}; none of it examines per-core sensor
presence.

\emph{We are not aware of prior work recovering per-die harvest maps from published out-of-band
telemetry, without access to the machines, or tracking them daily for years}, and that is the gap
this paper addresses. We state this as an awareness claim rather
than a priority claim: it rests on a systematic but necessarily incomplete search.


\section{Threats to validity and open issues}
\label{sec:threats}
Every item below carries the identifier it is tracked under in \texttt{LIMITATIONS.md} in the
artefact repository, which records for each one what is known, what would settle it, and where to
look; identifiers cited in the text but not itemised here (OI-14, OI-16) are recorded there too.
Nothing here is load-bearing for a headline result unless the entry says so.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Sensor presence $\ne$ core existence.} \textbf{Resolved externally for the count}
(Sec.~\ref{sec:groundtruth}): the operating system reports 128 logical CPUs on 99.9853\,\% of
847\,M samples, the scheduler's configured totals agree, and all nine OS departures from 128 are
matched by a BMC loss of that many cores, all independently of the IPMI sensor family. Neither
collector sees which positions are active; those rest on IBM's core numbering (OI-1).
\item \textbf{[OI-1] Resolved by documentation, with one link from a non-vendor source.} IBM's
chip diagram places the cores and labels the chiplets EC00--EC23~\cite[Fig.~2]{ibm_p9regs}: the two
slices of a quad are neighbours, index neighbours in different quads are not. That the per-core
sensors carry those numbers is reported in~\cite{smith2020p9layout} and is consistent with every
structural result here, none of which would survive a compact renumbering of the survivors.
Sec.~\ref{sec:layout} states what thermal coupling does and does not add; the diagram is a chip
diagram, not a to-scale die photograph, and a vendor statement or a second AC922 installation would
close the link.
\item \textbf{[OI-2] Bounded.} A March 2020 BMC firmware update could have remapped sensor IDs,
mimicking reconfiguration; 86 of the 235 transitions fall on 2020-03-20--21, though no single
slice permutation carries their old maps onto the new ones. Those days lie in the commissioning
window excluded in Sec.~\ref{par:commissioning}; maintenance logs would confirm the cause.
\item \textbf{[OI-4] Partly resolved.} Every reference has been checked against its publisher's
DOI record or the primary document, and each correction is logged in \texttt{LIMITATIONS.md}.
The \textbf{novelty check} stays open by nature: our claim is an awareness claim, resting on a
systematic but necessarily incomplete search, and the closest prior work we
found~\cite{mccalpin2021knl,smith2020p9layout} is discussed under Related work.
\item \textbf{[OI-3] Defect-driven versus policy-driven harvesting is not identified.} A
correlated defect process and a binning policy that disables contiguous blocks both predict
$\gamma>0$, and the one-$\gamma$ model is incomplete (Sec.~\ref{par:ppc}); an earlier draft's
claim that the fit showed ``both'' is withdrawn. A second installation, or vendor disclosure,
would be needed to separate the mechanisms.
\item \textbf{[OI-5] Resolved.} Monthly coverage ranges from 18\,\% to 99.4\,\%, but coverage is
uncorrelated with the harvest pattern and restricting to $\ge$90\,\% coverage leaves the lot
contrast unchanged (Sec.~\ref{sec:lot}).
\item \textbf{[OI-6, OI-7] both resolved.} The sub-16 socket-days were dominated by an
extraction defect on our side, now gated; the nine fully-sampled even-count days that survive are
the three strict guard episodes of Sec.~\ref{sec:lifecycle}, and the odd-count days are single-core
deconfigurations or single-sensor dropouts. The two nodes appearing to change within a day are
same-day reboots.
\item \textbf{[OI-8] Largely settled.} Of the relabelling events whose socket power difference
exceeds 2\,W on both sides, \textbf{12 of 15 flip sign} at the relabelling instant (95\,\% CI
$[52,96]$\,\%), as a collector-side tag swap predicts; per-socket longitudinal studies on this
dataset should still treat relabelling as a hazard.
\item \textbf{[OI-12] The guard episodes are a small count}: three meet the strict slice
criterion, and the nine slice or single-core deconfigurations in steady state are each matched by
the OS count and begin on a boot; the three strict ones are also corroborated by every other sensor
keeping full cadence. The GARD record itself is not in the telemetry.
\item \textbf{[OI-15] Pair-level counts depend on how the null treats the two lots.} The 16--18
significant pairs of earlier drafts (five seeds of a 200-draw null) are now 20 against 100{,}000
exchangeable draws. A null that keeps each lot's own marginals leaves 11, so the distant deficits in
particular are partly lot mixture; the near excesses (eight pooled, seven per lot) are the robust
part (Sec.~\ref{sec:cluster}).
\item \textbf{[OI-11] Common-mode artefact} in the thermal analysis, addressed by same-distance
contrasts and by ordering tests it cannot bias. The per-socket matrices, with the support of every
cell, are part of the artefact, and every thermal interval resamples the 49 racks.
\item \textbf{[OI-13] Analysis choices moved our numbers more often than the data did.} \emph{Five}
reported results changed materially during this work, none because of a new measurement.
\emph{Sample size:} a three-day sample overstated the within/cross-slice contrast more than
threefold ($+0.51$ vs $+0.29$, against $+0.372$ vs $+0.312$ over the full month) and reversed which
candidate layout scored best. \emph{Preprocessing:} an alignment grid, adopted to work around an
unrelated bug, inflated that contrast by $1.3\times$ at one minute and $2.2\times$ at 15 minutes
(Sec.~\ref{par:grid}). \emph{Bookkeeping:} an intermediate revision quoted a corrected figure from
a different subsample than the one it named. \emph{Uncertainty:} the claim that the map is
operationally invisible rested on an interval that treated node-days within a rack as independent;
corrected, the effect was not bounded below the margin, and the claim was withdrawn.
\emph{Specification:} fitting three collinear summaries of one map jointly, and scaling each
partial slope by its marginal range, nearly tripled the reported power effect ($+7.9$ against
$+2.7$\,W; Sec.~\ref{sec:impact}). \textbf{Four of the five were introduced while fixing or
extending something else, and the first four all ran in the direction we would have preferred.}
The defences that worked were mechanical: one stated pipeline for every number, varying each
discretionary choice (Sec.~\ref{sec:sens}), clustering uncertainty where the data are dependent, and
reporting the most conservative variant. Anyone mining a telemetry archive of this kind should be
at least as suspicious of their corrections as of their original analysis.
\item \textbf{[OI-10] Single system.} Findings may reflect CINECA's parts rather than POWER9
generally, although the same non-renumbered per-core sensors appear on another POWER9
platform~\cite{smith2020p9layout}; a second AC922 installation is the most valuable next test.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Conclusion}
Out-of-band telemetry that supercomputer operators already collect inadvertently publishes a
vendor-undisclosed attribute of every processor: which of its units are disabled. For 1{,}962
POWER9 sockets it shows that every disabled core in a map lies in a fully disabled slice, with no
exception on 1.52\,M clean socket-days; that the maps are per-die rather than per-SKU, with 443 of
495 possible patterns realised; that harvested slices cluster above all within the quads, slice
pairs adjacent in IBM's chip diagram, where thermal coupling is also strongest; and that a sharp
changepoint at rack~22 separates two hardware populations, most plausibly procurement lots,
placing 96\,\% of held-out racks in the right lot. Over 2.5 years the per-slice rates are stable to
within 0.25 percentage points per month, maps change rarely (0.21\,\% of cohort socket-months) and
at reboots (133 of 136 steady-state changes contain a boot, none is seen without one), and the nine
steady-state core-guard episodes each begin at a boot and match the OS's own CPU count.

What remains interpretation, pending vendor, service or delivery records, is why each position was
disabled, that the two populations are procurement lots, and that map changes are the events they
resemble. Two things deserve emphasis. Thermal coupling confirms the processor's quads from
telemetry alone, but beyond them it follows how the surviving cores are numbered, so the die layout
rests on IBM's documentation, not on telemetry. And the harvest map's operational footprint is now
\emph{bounded} rather than dismissed: over 636{,}026 paired node-days its association with socket
power is a few watts between the most dissimilar maps, and it does not usefully predict
replacement. These are bounded nulls we consider as useful as the positive ones, since they tell
operators what they can safely ignore and what they cannot yet.
The broader point is that which sensors exist, not what they read, can expose stable
component-level configuration and fleet cohort structure that aggregate telemetry would conceal,
and that those who publish such datasets should treat sensor identity as data.

\section*{Ethics and disclosure}
We used only the public M100 ExaData release (CC BY 4.0)~\cite{borghesi2023m100,m100_zenodo},
passively: no running system was accessed, no access control bypassed and no personal data used,
and Marconi100 left production in June 2023~\cite{cineca_m100_eol}. The maps disclose a
vendor-undisclosed configuration attribute and the fleet's cohort structure, not credentials or
access. A node's pair of maps is nearly unique in the fleet, so it could link records of the same
machine across datasets; the released tables carry only the dataset's own node identifiers.
Sec.~\ref{sec:channel} states which publication practices remove the channel.
